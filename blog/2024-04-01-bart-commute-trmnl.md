# Breaking the Doom Loop: BARTcom-TRMNL

Here's how the morning used to go: Wake up. Check phone. Open BART app. Refresh. Put phone down. Pick phone back up 30 seconds later. Refresh again. Still the same trains. Refresh once more just to be sure.

I was pulling the lever on a slot machine that rarely paid out. The information hadn't changed—trains don't magically appear between refreshes—but my brain couldn't resist checking. One more pull. Maybe this time.

The app was doing exactly what it was designed to do: keep me engaged. Each refresh was a little dopamine hit of activity, even when there was nothing new to see. I wasn't becoming more informed. I was just... refreshing.

Phone battery draining. Attention fragmenting. All for information that updates maybe once every few minutes but that I checked a dozen times.

## The Architecture of Compulsion

Modern smartphone transit apps are engagement machines. They don't care if you already know everything you need to know—they care about keeping you in the app. Transit apps on your phone refresh instantly because that feels responsive, feels active, feels like you're getting fresh information.

But you're not. You're getting the same information, repackaged with a satisfying swipe-down animation. The trains haven't moved. Nothing has changed. But the app successfully got you to engage again.

That's the doom loop: checking not because new information exists, but because checking feels good.

## The E-Ink Alternative

E-paper displays work differently. They only use power when updating the screen. This isn't a limitation—it's a philosophy embedded in the hardware.

When I discovered TRMNL, I realized: what if the display decided when to update, not me? What if it only refreshed when the information actually changed?

No more compulsive checking. No more pulling the lever. BARTcom runs on the TRMNL platform—the display shows the current trains, and it sits there, unchanged, until those trains are no longer current. When you glance at it and the information hasn't changed, that's not a bug—it's the system telling you "nothing new, you're good."

## Building for Intentionality

Creating BARTcom meant reversing every assumption I had about transit apps.

**Transit apps on your phone:** Update instantly when you refresh, whether anything changed or not.
**The plugin on e-paper:** Updates when trains actually change. Your compulsion to check can't make new information appear.

**Transit apps on your phone:** Live inside your pocket, demanding you decide to check them.
**The display on your desk:** Just... there. No decision required.

**Transit apps on your phone:** Optimized for engagement—every interaction is a win for the app.
**The TRMNL display:** Optimized for accuracy—only updates when accuracy requires it.

But here's the most important difference—one that perfectly embodies the intentional information philosophy:

**Transit apps show countdown timers:** "5 minutes... 3 minutes... 2 minutes..."
**BARTcom shows exact arrival times:** "7:42 AM, 7:58 AM, 8:15 AM"

This isn't a trivial choice. Countdown timers create urgency. They make you feel like time is running out. "Only 2 minutes!" triggers anxiety. You can't plan around a shrinking number—you can only react to it.

Exact arrival times let you make decisions. See "7:42 AM" and you know: you have time to make coffee, or you need to grab your keys now. The time doesn't change as you look at it. You don't need to do mental math. You don't need to refresh to see if the countdown is still accurate.

The e-paper constraint forced better design. I couldn't refresh constantly even if I wanted to, so I had to make each update count. Show the next few trains with their actual arrival times. Make it readable at a glance. Let it persist until it's worth replacing.

Multiple layouts exist not for variety, but because context matters. Your desk needs different information density than a wall mount. The platform doesn't care about engagement—it cares about getting you the right information for your situation.

## One Glance vs. Twelve Checks

My morning routine transformed. Instead of:
- Wake up → check phone → refresh → refresh → refresh → get dressed → check again → refresh → grab keys → one more refresh

It became:
- Wake up → glance at display → see 7:42, 7:58, 8:15 → make coffee or grab keys accordingly

The information is the same. But the relationship to that information completely changed. I'm not checking because I'm anxious or bored or because the app successfully trained me to check. I'm glancing because the information is right there, already current, requiring nothing from me.

When the display shows the same arrival times for several minutes, that's a feature. It means nothing has changed—the 7:42 train is still arriving at 7:42. I can trust it. I don't need to verify it. The TRMNL platform isn't trying to create engagement—it's trying to stay accurate for as long as possible.

## The Freedom of Not Checking

Phone battery lasts longer now. Not because I use it less overall, but because I'm not burning 30 minutes each morning pulling the lever of the transit slot machine.

More importantly: my attention lasts longer. I'm not fragmenting my morning into dozens of micro-checks. I glance once, get what I need, and move on with my day.

The trains still run on the same schedule. BART hasn't gotten more reliable. The information is identical to what transit apps on your phone would show.

But I've escaped the doom loop. The e-paper display now serves me information when it matters, not engagement when it doesn't.

That's the difference between platforms designed for your attention and platforms designed for your accuracy. E-paper can't do the engagement thing even if it wanted to—the hardware won't allow it. And that constraint creates freedom.
