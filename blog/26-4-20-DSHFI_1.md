# Deshitify #1: I Dusted Off a Gadget and Built Something That Works For Me

*This is the first post in Deshitify, a series about practical things you can do to take back your attention, your data, and your tech. It's inspired by Cory Doctorow's concept of enshittification: the process by which platforms and products slowly stop serving users and start extracting from them. Each entry has a project, a lesson, and something you can actually do, even if you never write a line of code.*

---

I am a certified gadget collector. I'm not proud of it.

I have drawers full of thingamajigs, doo-hickeys, and gizmos in various states of "I had big plans for this." Most of them sit in the dark, half-used and fully forgotten.

One of them was the LilyGo T5-S3 Pro. It looks kinda like an iPhone 4 (the best iPhone, don't @ me) except the screen is e-paper. Black and white. Low refresh rate. No notifications. It just... shows things. Quietly, patiently, like a very small corkboard that runs on a battery.

Out of the box, it does nothing. It's a development board, which is a polite way of saying it's a blank canvas that assumes you know what you're doing.

I didn't, really. But I fired up Claude Code and decided to figure it out.

## The Real Problem I Was Trying to Solve

The firmware I ended up building, [TRMNL-T5S3-firmware](https://github.com/jetsharklambo/TRMNL-T5S3-firmware), connects the T5-S3 to TRMNL, a platform designed around e-paper displays that shows you useful information without dragging you into a feed.

But the project wasn't really about firmware. It was about distraction.

If you've ever picked up your phone to check the time and put it down twenty minutes later having learned nothing useful, you already understand the problem. Your device isn't malfunctioning. It's working exactly as designed, just not for you.

Cory Doctorow calls this enshittification: the process by which platforms start out serving users, then pivot to extracting from them. You could say Google Search doesn't return what you asked for anymore. You could say Instagram stopped showing you your friends. You could say every app on your phone wants your location even when it has no earthly reason to. Most devices in your pocket are enshittification delivery mechanisms, optimized for engagement, which is a euphemism for compulsion.

An e-paper screen is structurally the opposite. It can't play video. It refreshes slowly. It doesn't ping you. It just sits there showing you something you decided you wanted to see.

That's the kind of device I actually wanted in my pocket.

## What I Actually Built

TRMNL is hardware and software that lets you design "plugins," which are simple displays of information you care about. Weather. Calendar. Transit times. A to-do list. The point is that you choose what's on the screen, and the screen never tries to show you something else.

The problem was that TRMNL's official firmware only ran on TRMNL's own hardware. My T5-S3 was sitting in a drawer being useless when it was perfectly capable of running the same thing.

So I wrote firmware that bridges the gap. Now the T5-S3 authenticates with TRMNL's server, downloads display images, renders them on the e-paper screen, and goes back to sleep. No notifications, no feed, no attention tax.

If you have a compatible device collecting dust, you can use this software to give it a job that works for you instead of against you.

## What Claude Code Taught Me About Software Development

At first, Claude Code felt kind of incredible. I could describe what I wanted and it would generate clean, elegant code faster than I could have written it myself. For a few hours it genuinely felt like the hard part was done.

Then I hit the wall.

The code was fine. The software development was not. I still had to understand the dependencies. I had to figure out what should be contributed upstream versus what should live in my fork. I had to make architecture decisions that would affect whether anyone else could trust or use this long term.

It's almost as if writing code and shipping software were two different jobs.

What got me through wasn't a better prompt. It was reaching out to real people. I talked to the maintainers of the libraries I was building on. I asked how they wanted contributions structured. I got perspective from developers who had already spent time understanding the ecosystem, the tradeoffs, and the community. That changed every major decision in the project.

Claude Code wrote my firmware. It didn't build my project.

## What You Can Do Right Now

Every Deshitify entry ends here, with something actionable at whatever level of effort you're willing to put in.

The theme of this first entry is simple: replace one attention-extracting screen with one that works for you. Glanceable ambient displays are the most tangible way to do that. They can't play video. They don't push notifications. They hold information without begging for your attention.

### Level 1: I don't wanna build it, I wanna buy it

Two solid options, depending on your aesthetic.

**[TRMNL](https://shop.trmnl.com/)** is a WiFi-connected e-paper display paired with a no-code dashboard builder. You pick from 500+ plugins and 8 layouts and build a dashboard of things you actually care about: weather, calendar, transit times, energy use, headlines from sources you trust. Then it sits there showing you that. No feed, no algorithm, no scroll.

**[Tidbyt](https://tidbyt.com)** is the pixel-art cousin. It's a small LED matrix instead of e-paper, so it's a different look (think retro arcade board on your shelf), but the philosophy is the same: glanceable, purposeful, not a feed. Apps for weather, stocks, transit, sports scores, whatever you want. If TRMNL is the quiet corkboard, Tidbyt is the little jukebox that minds its own business.

Either one is a real off-the-shelf answer to "I want a useful screen that isn't trying to hijack me."

### Level 2: You have a gadget collecting dust, use my firmware

If you already have a LilyGo T5-S3 sitting in a drawer, [TRMNL-T5S3-firmware](https://github.com/jetsharklambo/TRMNL-T5S3-firmware) connects it to the TRMNL ecosystem without needing to buy dedicated TRMNL hardware. Pair it with the [BYOD license](https://docs.trmnl.com/go/diy/byod) and you have a full TRMNL display for the cost of a development board.

That's the move that started this whole series. A gadget that did nothing, doing something useful.

### Level 3: Go deeper, build your own

You too can be an amateur firmware engineer. Claude Code handled the code generation; Perplexity was better at the "which library actually handles this protocol and is still maintained" research questions. Between the two, someone who understands hardware can work their way through it.

If you have a different gadget than mine collecting dust, you can get it running this stuff too. The tools are all there. The only question is what you want your screen to do for you.
